## Purpose

This policy establishes a unified approach to managing freshwater, wetlands, estuaries, and nearshore marine ecosystems as connected systems. It is designed to support biodiversity, climate resilience, Indigenous stewardship, and sustainable local economies while preventing cumulative ecological degradation.

---

## Relationship to Biodiversity Corridors and Rewilding

Wetlands, rivers, estuaries, and nearshore marine habitats function as **critical nodes and transition zones** within biodiversity corridor networks.

This policy directly complements the OCRP *Biodiversity Corridors & Rewilding Mandate* by:
- Designating aquatic ecosystems as corridor hubs and stepping stones
- Supporting species movement across land–freshwater–marine interfaces
- Reducing fragmentation caused by infrastructure, altered hydrology, and shoreline hardening

All projects under this policy must demonstrate how they contribute to ecological connectivity at the watershed or coastal scale.

---

## Scope

This policy applies to:
- Rivers, streams, lakes, floodplains, and inland wetlands
- Estuaries, tidal wetlands, and nearshore marine environments
- The land–water interface where terrestrial activities affect aquatic ecosystems

---

## Policy 1: Integrated Freshwater and Marine System Management

Freshwater and marine systems must be managed as **continuous ecological systems**, not separate regulatory domains.

Requirements:
1. Watershed and coastal planning must account for upstream, downstream, and land–sea interactions.
2. Estuaries and coastal wetlands must be treated as priority habitats for biodiversity, fisheries, and climate adaptation.
3. Land-based pollution, runoff, and sediment controls are required to protect downstream marine ecosystems.

---

## Policy 2: Invasive Species Prevention and Management

Invasive species are treated as a primary ecological risk.

Requirements:
1. Prevention measures must be applied to all projects involving soil movement, dredging, or equipment transfer between waterbodies.
2. Early detection and rapid response mechanisms must be established for priority invasive species.
3. Habitat restoration projects must include multi-year invasive species control and monitoring plans.
4. Marine pathways (for example vessel biofouling and ballast-related risks) must be addressed where applicable.

**Canadian example:**  
The **Great Lakes basin invasive species programs** demonstrate coordinated freshwater invasive management across jurisdictions, recognizing invasive species as a core driver of wetland and coastal habitat loss.

**International examples:**  
EU Invasive Alien Species Regulation; New Zealand Biosecurity framework.

---

## Policy 3: Erosion and Sediment Control as Habitat Protection

Sediment is treated as a pollutant and habitat stressor.

Requirements:
1. Erosion and sediment controls are mandatory for any land disturbance near water.
2. In-stream and nearshore works require enhanced sediment containment and post-construction verification.
3. Watershed plans must identify and address chronic sediment sources.
4. Enforcement mechanisms must include inspection authority and stop-work provisions.

**Canadian example:**  
British Columbia’s **Integrated Stormwater Management Planning** approach explicitly links erosion control, runoff management, and stream health protection.

---

## Policy 4: Habitat Restoration and Natural Processes

Restoration must prioritize ecological function over cosmetic outcomes.

Requirements:
1. Riparian and coastal buffers are treated as functional habitat and climate infrastructure.
2. Soft-engineering and nature-based solutions are the default for bank and shoreline stabilization.
3. Daylighting, floodplain reconnection, and wetland restoration must be prioritized where feasible.
4. Fish passage and species movement must be restored through barrier removal or redesign.

**Canadian example:**  
The **Still Creek restoration program (Metro Vancouver)** demonstrates urban stream daylighting, riparian restoration, invasive removal, and rainwater integration to support fish and wildlife recovery.

---

## Policy 5: Indigenous Communities and Co-Governance

Where Indigenous rights, title, or interests apply, governance must extend beyond consultation.

Requirements:
1. Establish co-governance or co-management pathways for priority waters and coastal areas.
2. Support Indigenous stewardship priorities and knowledge systems alongside scientific approaches.
3. Require proponents to demonstrate meaningful engagement and accommodation.

**Canadian examples:**
- **Gwaii Haanas Cooperative Management Board**, a joint federal–Haida governance model spanning terrestrial and marine ecosystems
- **Indigenous Protected and Conserved Areas (IPCAs)**, which provide a national framework for Indigenous-led conservation across land and water

**International examples:**  
New Zealand’s Te Mana o te Wai freshwater governance; Australia’s Sea Country and Indigenous Protected Areas.

---

## Policy 6: Community Involvement and Stewardship

Long-term ecosystem protection requires public participation and accountability.

Requirements:
1. Watershed and coastal plans must include structured public participation processes.
2. Citizen science and community monitoring programs are encouraged, with data quality standards.
3. Annual public reporting must describe ecological condition and trends, not only completed projects.

**Canadian example:**  
Community stewardship groups operating under watershed-based programs across Canada demonstrate the value of local monitoring and restoration partnerships when supported by clear policy direction.

---

## Policy 7: Climate Adaptation and Risk Reduction

Aquatic ecosystems provide natural protection against climate impacts.

Requirements:
1. Wetlands and floodplains must be protected and restored for flood attenuation and drought resilience.
2. Coastal wetlands and nearshore habitats must be used as nature-based defenses against storm surge and erosion.
3. Nature-based solutions are prioritized where they can replace or complement hard infrastructure.

---

## Policy 8: Tourism and Local Economic Co-Benefits

Healthy aquatic ecosystems support sustainable tourism and recreation.

Requirements:
1. Wetlands, rivers, and coastal habitats are recognized as public natural assets.
2. Access infrastructure must include carrying-capacity limits, seasonal protections, and habitat buffers.
3. Tourism activities must follow nature-positive management principles.
4. Where appropriate, tourism revenues may be reinvested in ecosystem management and monitoring.

Tourism is a conditional co-benefit. Ecological integrity remains the controlling objective.

---

## Monitoring, Reporting, and Adaptive Management

Requirements:
1. Monitoring must track ecological outcomes, not just project delivery.
2. Indicators may include water quality, habitat condition, species presence, and climate resilience metrics.
3. Results must be reported publicly and used to adapt management over time.

---

## Applicability

This policy is designed for adaptation by municipalities, regions, and other authorities responsible for land use, water management, coastal planning, or ecosystem restoration. It supports alignment with international biodiversity commitments while remaining implementable at local and regional scales.
